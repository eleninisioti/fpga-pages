Which exact circuit to deploy

The same function has many exact LUT configurations; the one the rule starts from decides whether it can repair it. ← circuits · method and caveats: docs/sat.md · data: overall_study/goldens/

The question

An FPGA compiler turns a target function into LUT contents deterministically — synthesis, then k-LUT technology mapping — but it chooses its own wiring and minimises area. Our wiring is fixed (the rule is tied to it), so the compiler's question becomes exact synthesis on a fixed topology, which a SAT solver answers exactly (soc.sat). That gave us a way to manufacture different exact circuits for one function and measure each as a starting point for repair — same rule, same wiring, same damage draws, only the golden differs.

Answer. The golden is most of the difference between a working and a failing deployment. A compiler-minimal circuit is exact and unrepairable; gradient descent leaves redundancy and does better; the circuit the rule repairs best is the one it grows itself. Cells are % of trials exact; hover for accuracy before → after.

adder8: let the compiler pick the wiring

Six bp campaigns on random wirings (768–1,280 gates, depth 8–12, 12k–36k iterations) plateaued at 0.978–0.9995 and the SAT finisher could not close them (fixing within the last 2 or 3 layers is UNSAT; wider is the hard regime again). soc.compile instead lets yosys/ABC choose the wiring: 22 LUT4 cells, depth 4, exact in milliseconds, embedded in a layered substrate with spare gates wired at random around it (pad 1x = 124 gates, 3x = 248). A rule trained on that substrate in three minutes repairs it — the table above. On the real chip routing bits are configured at deploy time exactly like LUT bits and the rule never rewrites routing, so this is place-and-route, not a cheat. Padding is the redundancy knob: the compiler-minimal circuit alone is the brittle case of section 3.

mul8: no exact circuit yet on the random wiring (bp 0.840, first run); the same compile route applies and is the next step.

What this page says about them once they exist: do not hand the rule the bp/SAT circuit as its golden — train a rule that grows adder8, and use the SAT-finished circuit as the teacher (--err-mode teacher) and for capacity questions.

Why: what the four adder2 circuits look like inside

All four compute the 2-bit adder exactly. The plain SAT circuit is what a compiler calls good: almost every bit zero, three quarters of the gates constant, a quarter of the bits ever read — and half of those bits break the adder if flipped. Gradient descent leaves redundancy: 80 % of single flips on bits the circuit reads are absorbed downstream. That redundancy is what repair routes through (the same reason synthesis needs (* keep *) to stop deleting the substrate).

What the solver costs

Exact synthesis on a random wiring is a hard instance, not a big one: the same clause count is instant when the solver starts near a solution. So SAT is a finisher for gradient descent or growth (--sticky: hold the reference's bits, release only what UNSAT cores force, re-pin in chunks) and an oracle — UNSAT is an answer bp can only report as a plateau — not a first step. The SAT ceiling rows above use it on hard errors: frozen cells as unit clauses, a complete solver over the rest. Where it says 100 % and the rule or the GA say 0 %, the task still fits in the free bits and the gap is search, not capacity; adder2 hits real UNSAT at 30–40 frozen gates (25 % / 62 % of draws).

The engineering answer

  1. Grow the initial circuit with the rule. On every circuit measured the rule's own grown configuration is the best golden for soft errors, hard errors and even the GA.
  2. Never deploy a compiler-minimal or unguided-SAT configuration as the golden: exact, and unrepairable.
  3. SAT-finishing a rule that cannot grow does not help (adder4, non-growing rule, above): not growing and not repairing are the same deficiency. bp + sticky SAT is the right generator of exact circuits for tasks with no rule yet (adder8, mul8) — for teacher goldens and capacity questions, not for deployment.
  4. Read every hard-error number against the SAT ceiling: it is the capacity wall.
