A hard error is a configuration cell that never takes a write again — aging or latch-up rather than a cosmic ray. It is the regime where the classical answers stop working by construction: a scrubber writes its golden copy back and the cells reject it, ECC decodes the right bits and cannot store them. Only a method that searches over the bits that still work has anything to do, and the rule never wanted the old bits back. boot means the cells were already dead when the rule started from random soft wires — can it assemble around them? repair means the rule grew a working circuit and only then did cells die.

What each method's solution costs on the die

Cells after place-and-route, per method, against how many gates were frozen. The gap is the finding: the climber flips the fewest cells it can, so most of the substrate stays constant and synthesis folds it away, while the rule re-solves through gates it would otherwise have left dead. A smaller number is not a better circuit here — the folded-away gates are exactly the redundancy a later repair would have to route through, which is why a compiler-minimal golden is the one nothing can repair.

Critical path: logic versus wire

The worst path on this die is 6.30 ns, of which 0.47 ns is the LUTs computing and 5.83 ns is getting between them. This is a static-timing result from place-and-route: every path examined, worst one reported. A testbench only drives the paths it happens to drive, so simulation can measure a delay but cannot prove a bound.

How long each input pattern takes to settle

Measured in the routed netlist with delays annotated — the output glitches as paths of different length arrive, and this is the last transition. The slowest pattern settles at 5.59 ns against the 6.30 ns static-timing bound above, and it should be under it: simulation finds what the testbench exercises, analysis proves what nothing exceeds.

What synthesis would fold away

60 gates in JAX become 35 cells with (* keep *) and 3 without it. A grown circuit is mostly dead or constant gates, and that redundancy is the substrate a repair rule re-routes through — so a build that lost the attribute would put a different circuit on the fabric from the one being maintained.

What ran on hardware, and what did not. This is the circuit the rule grew, on the real die with real delays. The rule that grew it ran as an integer datapath — soc2/hw/fixed.py, no float and no JAX anywhere in the loop, 8-bit weights and activations, a 2.4 KB image that fits in one block RAM from the bitstream. It reaches the same configurations the float rule does and then shuts its error gate. What has not happened yet is the RTL for that datapath, so the rule has not itself been placed and routed and simulated the way the circuit below has. hw/fixed.py is the executable specification it will be checked against. docs/refactoring.md section 7.
