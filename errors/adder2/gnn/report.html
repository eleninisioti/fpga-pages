Training is over and the weights are frozen; this is the rule doing what it does on the fabric, forward passes only. Three questions the training loss does not answer: does it converge from random starts, does it then stop writing, and does it land somewhere different each time.

Convergence

One thin line per trial (16 random starts), the thick line their mean. The spread is the point: a mean curve is a trajectory no single run ever traced.

When each trial became exact

Every trial reaching the target is one claim; reaching it at a predictable step is a different one.

Writes

Configuration bits rewritten per step, per trial. Reaching zero and staying there is the claim that matters: the rule can be switched off and what remains is an ordinary combinational circuit.

Persistence

Rolled to 1024 steps -- far past the training horizon, because a rule that is merely slow looks identical to one that has settled if you stop looking at step 64. Mean over trials.

Persistence: writes

The same run. Separate axes on purpose -- a fraction and a count share no scale, and putting them together flattens whichever has the smaller range onto the floor.

Pairwise distance is the diversity claim on its own: near zero means the rule prints one memorised configuration, while a spread of distinct configurations that are all exact means it re-solves. The substrate id is the wiring these trials ran on, read from the rule itself — a deployed rule cannot be run on a circuit other than the one it was trained on.

Exactness, per seed

What fraction of random starts each rule takes all the way to the truth table. This is the reproducibility claim, and it is a count of rules rather than of starts.

Distance is two numbers once there is more than one rule: 0.0131 between the configurations one seed reaches from different starts, and 0.3371 between the configurations different seeds reach (13 against 324 bits of 960). The first is a rule re-solving; the second is whether the training run, rather than the task, chose the circuit.

Where it landed on the die

Every placed cell, at the coordinates nextpnr chose, on the whole 128×95 tile grid rather than a crop around the design — the point is how little of this chip a 60-gate circuit is. Hover a cell for its site. Gates that the layer diagram draws as neighbours are tens of tiles apart here, which is the next figure's explanation.

Critical path: logic versus wire

The worst path on this die is 6.73 ns, of which 0.24 ns is the LUTs computing and 6.49 ns is getting between them. This is a static-timing result from place-and-route: every path examined, worst one reported. A testbench only drives the paths it happens to drive, so simulation can measure a delay but cannot prove a bound.

How long each input pattern takes to settle

Measured in the routed netlist with delays annotated — the output glitches as paths of different length arrive, and this is the last transition. The slowest pattern settles at 6.00 ns against the 6.73 ns static-timing bound above, and it should be under it: simulation finds what the testbench exercises, analysis proves what nothing exceeds.

What synthesis would fold away

60 gates in JAX become 43 cells with (* keep *) and 3 without it. A grown circuit is mostly dead or constant gates, and that redundancy is the substrate a repair rule re-routes through — so a build that lost the attribute would put a different circuit on the fabric from the one being maintained.

What ran on hardware, and what did not. This is the circuit the rule grew, on the real die with real delays. The rule that grew it ran as an integer datapath — soc2/hw/fixed.py, no float and no JAX anywhere in the loop, 8-bit weights and activations, a 2.4 KB image that fits in one block RAM from the bitstream. It reaches the same configurations the float rule does and then shuts its error gate. What has not happened yet is the RTL for that datapath, so the rule has not itself been placed and routed and simulated the way the circuit below has. hw/fixed.py is the executable specification it will be checked against. docs/refactoring.md section 7.
